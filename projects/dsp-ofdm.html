Digital Signal Processing & OFDM

Design and analysis of digital filters, inverse filtering, multipath-channel equalization and an OFDM communication system implemented in MATLAB.

DSP FIR / IIR OFDM MATLAB Digital Communications

OVERVIEW

From filter design to multicarrier communication

This project combined several core digital signal processing concepts. The first part focused on frequency-domain analysis, FIR and IIR filter design, disturbance removal and inverse filtering of echo-corrupted audio.

The second part applied the same system perspective to a digital communication channel with multipath propagation. Equalization was first studied directly in the time domain before implementing an OFDM system using the DFT, IDFT, cyclic prefix and frequency-domain equalization.

01

FIR & IIR Notch Filtering

A tonal disturbance at one quarter of the sampling frequency was removed by placing zeros at the corresponding points on the unit circle:

z = ±j

This resulted in a second-order FIR notch filter with zero gain at the disturbance frequency. The filter successfully removed the unwanted component, but also attenuated nearby frequencies and therefore affected the perceived audio quality.

Narrowing the notch with an IIR filter

A second-order IIR filter was then designed by placing poles close to the zeros at a radius of 0.95. The nearby poles compensate for attenuation away from the exact disturbance frequency, producing a much narrower notch.

FIR

Zeros only

Completely removes the tonal disturbance, but noticeably attenuates nearby frequencies.

VS
IIR

Zeros + nearby poles

Maintains the notch while reducing the effect on neighbouring frequencies.

Key observation

Both filters removed the disturbance, but the IIR notch filter preserved the surrounding audio spectrum more effectively.

02

Audio Signal Processing

Audio signals were analysed in both the time and frequency domains. A moving-average filter was used to demonstrate the relationship between time-domain smoothing and attenuation of high-frequency components.

Experiments were also performed with additive white Gaussian noise. The moving-average filter reduced high-frequency content but was not effective as a general noise-removal method because the noise energy was spread across the spectrum.

03

Echo Modelling & Inverse Filtering

An echo was modelled as a delayed and attenuated copy of the original signal added to the direct signal:

y(n) = x(n) + αx(n − D)

The corresponding system function is:

H(z) = 1 + αz−D

If the echo system is known, its effect can be cancelled by applying the inverse system:

Hinv(z) = 1 / (1 + αz−D)

The inverse filter was tested on a speech recording where a delayed reflection was introduced. After filtering, the recovered waveform matched the original signal closely and the echo was no longer audible.

Stability

The inverse filter poles have magnitude |α|1/D. The inverse system is therefore unstable when |α| > 1.

04

Multipath Communication Channel

The communication part modelled a channel containing a direct signal and one delayed reflection. With a one-sample delay, the received signal was described by:

r(n) = s(n) + αs(n − 1)

giving the channel system function:

H(z) = 1 + αz−1

Binary data was mapped to ±1 symbols and transmitted through the channel for several reflection coefficients. Without noise, the original bits could be recovered from the sign of the received samples.

Adding Gaussian noise exposed a weakness of this simple detection strategy. For the most severe tested channel, α = −0.99, the received samples could approach zero and noise could change their sign, producing bit errors.

05

Channel Equalization

If the channel response is known, its effect can theoretically be removed by applying the inverse channel response:

Heq(z) = 1 / H(z)

Both transmitter-side and receiver-side processing were investigated. In the noise-free case, both methods restored the transmitted signal.

Receiver equalization is more practical when the channel must be estimated from the received signal. However, noise is also present at the receiver and can be affected by the inverse filter, making direct equalization less robust for difficult channel conditions.

06

OFDM & Frequency-Domain Equalization

The final part implemented an OFDM system. Instead of equalizing the full channel directly in the time domain, the transmitted block was transformed using an inverse DFT.

05 DFT + equalization

Remove the channel independently in each frequency bin.

The cyclic prefix converts the relevant part of the channel operation into circular convolution. After applying the DFT, convolution becomes multiplication:

Y(k) = H(k)X(k)

Equalization can therefore be performed independently for each frequency component:

X̂(k) = Y(k) / H(k)

07

Noise Robustness & Bit Recovery

The OFDM implementation was tested using the same channel conditions as the earlier direct equalization experiments. White Gaussian noise was added before bit recovery.

Method Channel condition Observation
Direct receiver processing α = −0.99 + noise Bit errors occurred
OFDM equalization All tested α values + noise Bits recovered without errors

In the reported experiment, the OFDM approach recovered the transmitted bits without errors for all tested reflection coefficients at the chosen noise level. This demonstrated the advantage of converting the multipath channel into frequency-domain scalar subchannels before equalization.
